HUD fair market rent by metro, FY2027: the 2BR medians of 387 CBSA complexes

FY2027 edition, published 2026-09-10. Data source: HUD FY2027 Fair Market Rent release, ZIP-level file parsed from FY27_FMRs.xlsx plus the FY2027 small-area FMR file (FY27_safmrs.xlsx), rates effective October 1, 2026. The short answer

Group HUD's FY2027 ZIP file by the five-digit CBSA (core-based statistical area) number embedded in every metro area code and 649 metro area codes collapse into 393 CBSA complexes. Rank the 387 that survive Puerto Rico's exclusion by the median 2BR fair market rent of their ZIP rows, and the board spans 3,330 dollars: Santa Cruz-Watsonville, California tops it at 4,250, 4.6 times the 920-dollar floor Dothan and Gadsden share in Alabama. The middle 80 percent of complex medians run from 1,043 to 1,980 dollars around a 1,290-dollar midpoint. Nine of the ten most expensive complexes sit in California; the cheapest ten scatter across Alabama, Louisiana, West Virginia, Oklahoma, Arkansas and Missouri.

Each complex median is recomputed from the deduped ZIP rows inside the complex; HUD's published FMR for an area is its own 40th-percentile estimate and can differ. No population weighting trims the small end, so 16-ZIP Napa ranks beside 615-ZIP Los Angeles. Both boards recompute from the CSV behind the FY2027 ZIP dataset, and the two SQL blocks at the end of this page return them verbatim.

Methodology: complexes, dedup, medians

The source is HUD's FY2027 ZIP-level fair market rent file, 51,871 rows parsed from FY27_FMRs.xlsx plus the FY2027 small-area FMR file (FY27_safmrs.xlsx), rates effective October 1, 2026, public domain. The 27,288 rows under METRO area codes form the metro universe; Puerto Rico's 193 metro rows across 11 areas are excluded, the treatment every ranking page in this series applies. Complex membership comes from the five digits after METRO in the area code, so 649 metro area codes collapse into 393 complexes, six of them entirely Puerto Rican, leaving 387 ranked complexes.

Within a complex, 129 ZIP codes repeat under two or more area codes, contributing 1,049 surplus rows, and every repeat carries identical rates across all five bedroom sizes, so the duplicates drop out without loss and 26,046 unique ZIP values remain. Each complex median is the middle value, or the average of the two middle values where the count is even (197 of the 387 complexes). Every 2BR rate in the file moves in 5-dollar steps, so every complex median lands on a multiple of 5. Percentiles interpolate linearly between the two nearest order statistics: the 10th percentile of the 387 medians sits at position 38.6, hence 1,043. These medians are recomputed from ZIP rows, not HUD's published area FMRs, which are 40th-percentile estimates; no population weighting is applied; HUD can revise FY2027 mid-cycle. This page documents the data; it is not housing or legal advice.

Recompute the boards yourself

Load the FY2027 ZIP file into SQLite as zips_fy2027, ten columns per row, following the import steps in the SQL guide. The two boards need one grouping and one median each:

-- SQLite: the ten most expensive CBSA complexes, FY2027
CREATE TABLE zips_fy2027 (zip TEXT, hud_area_code TEXT, metro TEXT,
  area_name TEXT, state TEXT, fmr_0br INT, fmr_1br INT,
  fmr_2br INT, fmr_3br INT, fmr_4br INT);
.import --skip 1 fmr-by-zip-2027.csv zips_fy2027

-- complex = every area code sharing the five digits after METRO;
-- repeated ZIPs inside a complex carry identical values, so MIN dedups
WITH m AS (
  SELECT zip, hud_area_code, area_name, state, CAST(fmr_2br AS INT) AS v,
         SUBSTR(hud_area_code, 6, 5) AS cbsa,
         ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY SUBSTR(hud_area_code, 6, 5), zip
                            ORDER BY hud_area_code) AS rn
  FROM zips_fy2027
  WHERE hud_area_code LIKE 'METRO%' AND state <> 'PR'
),
d AS (SELECT cbsa, zip, v FROM m WHERE rn = 1),
r AS (SELECT cbsa, v, ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY cbsa ORDER BY v) AS rn,
             COUNT(*) OVER (PARTITION BY cbsa) AS n FROM d),
med AS (SELECT cbsa, AVG(v) AS med FROM r WHERE rn IN ((n+1)/2, (n+2)/2) GROUP BY cbsa),
cnt AS (SELECT cbsa, COUNT(*) AS zip_rows FROM r GROUP BY cbsa),
areas AS (SELECT cbsa, COUNT(DISTINCT hud_area_code) AS areas FROM m GROUP BY cbsa),
lab AS (SELECT cbsa, area_name, state,
               ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY cbsa ORDER BY k DESC, area_name) AS rn
        FROM (SELECT cbsa, area_name, state, COUNT(*) AS k
              FROM m GROUP BY cbsa, area_name, state))
SELECT med.cbsa, lab.area_name, lab.state, cnt.zip_rows,
       ROUND(med.med, 2) AS med2br
FROM med JOIN cnt ON cnt.cbsa = med.cbsa
         JOIN areas ON areas.cbsa = med.cbsa
         JOIN lab ON lab.cbsa = med.cbsa AND lab.rn = 1
ORDER BY med.med DESC, med.cbsa
LIMIT 10;

The percentiles, the shape counts, and the split-complex gaps come out of the same table:

-- SQLite: percentiles of the 387 complex medians, linear interpolation
WITH m AS (
  SELECT zip, hud_area_code, CAST(fmr_2br AS INT) AS v,
         SUBSTR(hud_area_code, 6, 5) AS cbsa,
         ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY SUBSTR(hud_area_code, 6, 5), zip
                            ORDER BY hud_area_code) AS rn
  FROM zips_fy2027
  WHERE hud_area_code LIKE 'METRO%' AND state <> 'PR'
),
d AS (SELECT cbsa, zip, v FROM m WHERE rn = 1),
r AS (SELECT cbsa, v, ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY cbsa ORDER BY v) AS rn,
             COUNT(*) OVER (PARTITION BY cbsa) AS n FROM d),
med AS (SELECT cbsa, AVG(v) AS med FROM r WHERE rn IN ((n+1)/2, (n+2)/2) GROUP BY cbsa),
p AS (SELECT med AS v, ROW_NUMBER() OVER (ORDER BY med) AS rn,
             COUNT(*) OVER () AS n FROM med)
SELECT
  ROUND((SELECT a.v + (0.10*(c.n-1) - CAST(0.10*(c.n-1) AS INTEGER)) * (b.v - a.v)
         FROM p c, p a, p b
         WHERE a.rn = CAST(0.10*(c.n-1) AS INTEGER) + 1
           AND b.rn = CAST(0.10*(c.n-1) AS INTEGER) + 2 LIMIT 1), 2) AS p10,
  ROUND((SELECT a.v + (0.50*(c.n-1) - CAST(0.50*(c.n-1) AS INTEGER)) * (b.v - a.v)
         FROM p c, p a, p b
         WHERE a.rn = CAST(0.50*(c.n-1) AS INTEGER) + 1
           AND b.rn = CAST(0.50*(c.n-1) AS INTEGER) + 2 LIMIT 1), 2) AS p50,
  ROUND((SELECT a.v + (0.90*(c.n-1) - CAST(0.90*(c.n-1) AS INTEGER)) * (b.v - a.v)
         FROM p c, p a, p b
         WHERE a.rn = CAST(0.90*(c.n-1) AS INTEGER) + 1
           AND b.rn = CAST(0.90*(c.n-1) AS INTEGER) + 2 LIMIT 1), 2) AS p90,
  (SELECT MAX(v) FROM p) - (SELECT MIN(v) FROM p) AS span,
  (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM p) AS complexes;
-- 1043.0 / 1290.0 / 1980.0 / 3330.0 / 387.

-- shape counts
WITH m AS (
  SELECT zip, hud_area_code, CAST(fmr_2br AS INT) AS v,
         SUBSTR(hud_area_code, 6, 5) AS cbsa,
         ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY SUBSTR(hud_area_code, 6, 5), zip
                            ORDER BY hud_area_code) AS rn
  FROM zips_fy2027
  WHERE hud_area_code LIKE 'METRO%' AND state <> 'PR'
),
d AS (SELECT cbsa, zip, v FROM m WHERE rn = 1),
r AS (SELECT cbsa, v, ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY cbsa ORDER BY v) AS rn,
             COUNT(*) OVER (PARTITION BY cbsa) AS n FROM d),
med AS (SELECT cbsa, AVG(v) AS med FROM r WHERE rn IN ((n+1)/2, (n+2)/2) GROUP BY cbsa)
SELECT
  (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM med) AS complexes,
  (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM med WHERE med >= 2000) AS at_or_above_2000,
  (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM med WHERE med <= 1100) AS at_or_below_1100,
  (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM (
     SELECT cbsa FROM m GROUP BY cbsa HAVING COUNT(DISTINCT hud_area_code) > 1)) AS split_complexes,
  (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM med WHERE CAST(med AS INT) <> med) AS noninteger_medians;
-- 387 / 37 / 81 / 141 / 0.

-- the three widest split-complex gaps, one row per sub-area median
WITH m AS (
  SELECT zip, hud_area_code, area_name, CAST(fmr_2br AS INT) AS v,
         SUBSTR(hud_area_code, 6, 5) AS cbsa,
         ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY hud_area_code, zip
                            ORDER BY hud_area_code) AS rn
  FROM zips_fy2027
  WHERE hud_area_code LIKE 'METRO%' AND state <> 'PR'
),
d AS (SELECT cbsa, hud_area_code, area_name, zip, v FROM m WHERE rn = 1),
r AS (SELECT hud_area_code, area_name, cbsa, v,
             ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY hud_area_code ORDER BY v) AS rn,
             COUNT(*) OVER (PARTITION BY hud_area_code) AS n FROM d),
am AS (SELECT cbsa, hud_area_code, area_name, AVG(v) AS med

SELECT sp.gap, am.cbsa, am.area_name, ROUND(am.med, 2) AS med
FROM sp JOIN am ON am.cbsa = sp.cbsa
ORDER BY sp.gap DESC, am.med DESC, am.hud_area_code;

Metro medians by CBSA, FY2027: questions

Which metro area has the highest median 2BR fair market rent in FY2027?
The Santa Cruz-Watsonville, California complex tops the 387 ranked CBSA complexes at a 4,250-dollar median for a 2BR, computed across its 23 ZIP values. San Jose-Sunnyvale-Santa Clara follows at 3,270 and the Oakland-Fremont complex at 3,255, the complex whose area codes also carry San Francisco, where the sub-area median runs 3,590. Nine of the ten most expensive complexes sit in California; New York, at 2,790 dollars across its seven New York and New Jersey sub-areas, is the only exception.
Which metro areas are cheapest for a 2BR in FY2027?
Dothan, Alabama and Gadsden, Alabama tie at a 920-dollar median, the lowest of the 387 ranked CBSA complexes. Lawton, Oklahoma follows at 930, Decatur, Alabama at 950, Monroe, Louisiana and Parkersburg-Vienna, West Virginia at 960, Alexandria, Louisiana at 965, and a three-way tie at 970 between Raleigh County, West Virginia, Fort Smith, Arkansas-Oklahoma and Jefferson City, Missouri. The board concentrates in the Deep South and West Virginia: three Alabama complexes, two Louisiana and two West Virginia, plus Oklahoma, Arkansas and Missouri.
How were the CBSA rankings computed?
From HUD's FY2027 ZIP-level file, 51,871 rows, rates effective October 1, 2026. Every row under a METRO area code shares its five-digit CBSA number with the rest of its complex, so 649 metro area codes collapse into 393 complexes; Puerto Rico's 193 metro rows (11 areas, 6 complexes) are excluded, leaving 387 complexes ranked on 26,046 unique ZIP values after removing 1,049 duplicate rows that repeat identical rates. Each complex median is the middle value, or the average of the two middle values on even counts, and the national percentiles interpolate linearly.
