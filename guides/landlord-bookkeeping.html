Landlord bookkeeping: how to track rental income and expenses

Published 2026-09-11. This page documents bookkeeping practice for US residential landlords; it is not tax, legal, or accounting advice, and a tax professional handles your specific filing.

A tenant pays rent on the first. In March the water heater dies and you pay the plumber. In April you pay the insurance premium and the county tax bill. At filing time, which of those four payments cuts your tax bill, and which one lands on a depreciation schedule instead? The answer lives in your bookkeeping, and bookkeeping for a rental is a smaller job than it looks if you set it up around two things: the income you must count, and the expense categories the tax form already uses.

Count all the income, including the awkward parts

Rent is the obvious line. Three more kinds of money belong on it.

Late fees count as rental income. Rent paid in advance counts in the year you receive it, so December rent collected in December belongs to this year even if it covers January. A security deposit you plan to return is not income when it arrives, but the part you keep, for damage beyond normal wear or for unpaid rent, becomes income when you keep it. Application fees and pet fees fall in the same bucket: money you collect because you own the unit.

If you file on a cash basis, which most individual landlords do, income is counted when received. That timing rule settles most "which year does this go in" questions on its own.

Use the expense categories the tax form already has

Schedule E (Form 1040), Part I, is where rental income and expenses get reported, and its expense lines are a ready-made chart of accounts. The categories on the form:

Two habits fall out of that table. First, tag every expense with the Schedule E line when you record it, and tax preparation turns into adding up columns instead of re-sorting a year of bank transactions. Second, keep tenant-paid utilities off the expense list; you cannot deduct a bill the rent covers.

The spreadsheet that carries it

A rental property bookkeeping spreadsheet needs four things per expense row: date, payee, category, and amount. Add payment method and a notes column, and a link or filename for the receipt. One tab for income, one for expenses, one tab per property if you own more than one.

The income tab wants the same discipline: date, unit, tenant or source, amount, and what it was (rent, late fee, kept deposit). A rent roll sits naturally beside it, one row per unit with status, monthly rent, and occupancy, so vacancy shows up as a number rather than a feeling.

Totals do the reporting work. A monthly expense total per category, an annual column that sums the months, and income minus expenses at the bottom. That bottom number, net operating income before mortgage principal and depreciation, is also the number that tells you whether the property performs. A simple example: rent of 1,650 a month against 7,900 of yearly operating expenses and taxes leaves about 11,900 before debt service. If the annual column never gets built, that comparison never happens until a tax form forces it.

Keep a separate bank account for the property. Commingled personal and rental money is where mixed-up books start, and untangling a year of shared transactions costs more than the account does.

Repairs, improvements, and the line between them

The plumber invoice and a new roof are both "money spent on the building", and the tax treatment splits them. A repair restores something to its normal working condition and is deducted in the year you pay it. An improvement extends the life of the property, adds a new feature, or adapts it to a new use, and it is recovered through depreciation instead. Residential rental buildings depreciate over 27.5 years in the US, and the land under them does not depreciate at all.

Record which is which at the time you pay, with the invoice. A category note that says "repair" or "improvement" in March is worth more than reconstructing the decision from memory two years later. The IRS general rule for record retention is about three years from filing, which is also the horizon for keeping receipts.

The monthly habit: record transactions weekly or monthly, while receipts are still findable.
