Open Street Map civic data: fountains, bookcases, and bike repair stations in 16 cities

OpenStreetMap tags three kinds of street-level civic infrastructure that most map apps skip: amenity=drinking_water (fountains and public taps), amenity=public_bookcase (the little street-side shelves where neighbors leave and take books), and amenity=bicycle_repair_station (public stands with tools and an air pump). If you have ever searched for a public bookcase map or a bike repair station map and come up empty, the data exists in OSM; what is missing is the summary layer. This guide counts those three tags across 16 cities in four regions and hands over the numbers as free CSV. The catalog now covers seven regions on the same snapshot date, 41,014 features in total; the worldwide 19-city table adds Sao Paulo, Buenos Aires, and Mexico City, and the Istanbul-Cairo-Mumbai guide covers its own region.

Where the numbers surprise

Seattle owns the bookcase count. Its 497 public bookcases are more than double second-place New York City (221) and about 37% of every bookcase in the set combined, which tracks with the Little Free Library movement being strongest in the US Pacific Northwest. The EU peak is Paris at 165, and the Asia numbers stay in single digits because the public_bookcase tag never caught on there; Amsterdam's 0 is the same story, a mapping-culture gap rather than an absence of shelves.

Nairobi maps 2,174 drinking-water points, the largest count in the Africa file and the second-largest single-city fountain count in the seven-region series: Sydney's 2,479 in the Oceania file holds the top spot, with Nairobi ahead of Tokyo (2,163) and Madrid (2,058). Almost all of them are unnamed tap points, with a dense cluster mapped by water and sanitation projects in Kibera; Lagos adds 60 and Addis Ababa 5. The Africa bookcase column reads 0 in all three cities and the repair column holds a single stand, a gitonga bicycle services stand in Nairobi. Whether the zeros are a tagging gap or an infrastructure gap is the same question Amsterdam's 0 asks, and only fieldwork settles it.

Fountains concentrate in a few capitals. Madrid maps 2,058 drinking-water nodes, Paris adds 1,045, and Tokyo's 2,163 used to top the whole table before Nairobi passed it. Bike repair stations flip the pattern: Warsaw's 188 stands top every other city, and Berlin's 71 is the only other count above 50.

How the counts were queried

All counts ran on 2026-09-10 against the Overpass API, so they are snapshots of a database that changes daily. The EU and Asia counts filter by administrative boundary: Nominatim relation lookups (Paris R71525, Madrid R5326784, Tokyo R1543125, and the rest) converted to Overpass area IDs, then one count query per amenity per city, so the numbers follow exact city limits. The US counts filter by city bounding box, close enough for the five cities chosen because their boxes stay inside the city proper. The Africa counts went back to admin boundaries: Lagos State (R3718182), Nairobi County (R3492709), and the Addis Ababa city administration (R1707699), each converted to an Overpass area ID the same way. The Asia counts file also carries one example location per amenity, with the name tag, latitude, and longitude; the US, EU, and Africa files are count-only.

The Oceania counts use a different trio

The seventh region swaps bookcases and repair stations for amenity=toilets and amenity=bench, so its rows do not fit the table above. Sydney counts 2,479 fountains, 2,501 public toilets, and 13,573 benches under the metro place boundary; Melbourne counts 355 fountains, 142 toilets, and 1,845 benches inside the City of Melbourne only, the 37 square kilometer core municipality rather than greater Melbourne; Auckland counts 428 fountains, 992 toilets, and 4,916 benches under the unitary authority. That is 27,231 features in one region, more than five times the rest of the set combined, and Sydney's bench column, 13,573, is second in the catalog only to New York's 18,991 benches in the exact-boundary US file. The trio is also the series' first look at benches, a tag that scales differently from bookcases because every park contributes. Full rows live in the Sydney-Melbourne-Auckland dataset with the CSV free to download.

From counts to a public bookcase map or bike repair station map

A per-city count answers a different question than a map does. Use the counts to compare civic infrastructure coverage between cities, to pick which city deserves a deeper dive, or to sanity-check a claim like "my city has the most little libraries." A count table also gives an LLM or a data pipeline something a slippy map cannot: one row per city, stable columns, no tiles to render.

When you do need the map, the counts tell you whether building one is worth it. Seattle's 497 bookcases mean a public bookcase map for that city has real content; Jakarta's 0 says the map would be blank today, or that someone should go tag the shelves first. The same logic holds for a bike repair station map, where Warsaw's 188 stands and Madrid's 3 sit at opposite ends. To go from counts to coordinates, query Overpass yourself with the same amenity tags inside one city area; the Asia dataset's example locations show what one row of that output looks like.
