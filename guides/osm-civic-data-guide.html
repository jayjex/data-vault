Open Street Map civic data: fountains, bookcases, and bike repair stations in 13 cities

OpenStreetMap tags three kinds of street-level civic infrastructure that most map apps skip: amenity=drinking_water (fountains and public taps), amenity=public_bookcase (the little street-side shelves where neighbors leave and take books), and amenity=bicycle_repair_station (public stands with tools and an air pump). If you have ever searched for a public bookcase map or a bike repair station map and come up empty, the data exists in OSM; what is missing is the summary layer. This guide counts all three tags across 13 cities on three continents and hands over the numbers as free CSV.

13 cities, three amenity tags, 11,158 nodes

The count set covers five US cities, five EU cities, and three Asian cities. Every number below is the count of OSM nodes carrying that amenity tag inside the city:

Where the numbers surprise

Seattle owns the bookcase count. Its 497 public bookcases are more than double second-place New York City (221) and about 37% of every bookcase in the set combined, which tracks with the Little Free Library movement being strongest in the US Pacific Northwest. The EU peak is Paris at 165, and the Asia numbers stay in single digits because the public_bookcase tag never caught on there; Amsterdam's 0 is the same story, a mapping-culture gap rather than an absence of shelves.

Fountains concentrate in two capitals. Madrid maps 2,058 drinking-water nodes, and Paris adds 1,045; between them they hold a third of the set's fountains, consistent with the public-fountain networks both cities maintain. Tokyo's 2,163 drinking-water points top the whole table. Bike repair stations flip the pattern: Warsaw's 188 stands beat the other twelve cities combined, and Madrid maps 3.

How the counts were queried

All counts ran on 2026-09-10 against the Overpass API, so they are snapshots of a database that changes daily. The EU and Asia counts filter by administrative boundary: Nominatim relation lookups (Paris R71525, Madrid R5326784, Tokyo R1543125, and the rest) converted to Overpass area IDs, then one count query per amenity per city, so the numbers follow exact city limits. The US counts filter by city bounding box, close enough for the five cities chosen because their boxes stay inside the city proper. The Asia counts file also carries one example location per amenity, with the name tag, latitude, and longitude; the US and EU files are count-only.

From counts to a public bookcase map or bike repair station map

A per-city count answers a different question than a map does. Use the counts to compare civic infrastructure coverage between cities, to pick which city deserves a deeper dive, or to sanity-check a claim like "my city has the most little libraries." A count table also gives an LLM or a data pipeline something a slippy map cannot: one row per city, stable columns, no tiles to render.

When you do need the map, the counts tell you whether building one is worth it. Seattle's 497 bookcases mean a public bookcase map for that city has real content; Jakarta's 0 says the map would be blank today, or that someone should go tag the shelves first. The same logic holds for a bike repair station map, where Warsaw's 188 stands and Madrid's 3 sit at opposite ends. To go from counts to coordinates, query Overpass yourself with the same amenity tags inside one city area; the Asia dataset's example locations show what one row of that output looks like.
