How much rent can I afford? The 30% rule, tested on real rent data

Published 2026-09-10. Data sources: HUD FY2027 Fair Market Rent file (CSVs on this site and Zenodo), public domain; US Census Bureau, 2024 American Community Survey one-year estimates via Census Reporter, retrieved 2026-09-10, public domain. This page is data and arithmetic; it is not financial, housing, or legal advice. Data Vault is not affiliated with HUD or the Census Bureau.

The rule and the ratio

The rent-to-income ratio divides monthly rent by gross monthly income. A ratio of 0.30 means rent takes 30 cents of every earned dollar. The 30% rule treats that as the ceiling: everything above it counts as cost-burdened. Two lines of arithmetic give the same answer:

rent budget      = gross monthly income × 0.30
income needed    = monthly rent × 40

The second line is the first one inverted, with the division already done. A rent of 1,190 needs 47,600 of annual income to sit at 30%.

The 30% line has a federal paper trail. The Brooke Amendment of 1969 capped public housing rents at 25% of a tenant's income, and Congress raised the standard to 30% in 1981. HUD still counts any household paying more than 30% of income for housing as cost-burdened, and in the Housing Choice Voucher program the family share can never exceed 40% of adjusted income at the initial lease when gross rent tops the payment standard, per 24 CFR 982.505. The mechanics of the voucher math are on the Section 8 voucher rent page, and the pure formula side of affordability calculations, worked against FY2026 data, is on the rent affordability calculator page.

Two rents to test against

Can I afford this rent has two published benchmarks, and they answer differently. The HUD fair market rent is a 40th percentile gross rent, shelter rent plus utilities, for standard-quality units rented by recent movers in each area, refreshed every federal fiscal year. The ACS median gross rent is the 50th percentile across every renter-occupied unit paying cash rent, long-tenured leases included, on the same rent-plus-utilities basis. What fair market rent is walks the FMR definition; HUD FMR vs market rent shows why the two orderings can run either way, San Diego worked example included.

Nationally, the 2024 ACS 2BR median gross rent is 1,490, and the FY2026 2BR FMR median across all 51,895 ZIP rows was 1,150. The FY2027 file, effective October 1, 2026, moved that median to 1,190, a +3.30% change documented in the FY2027 FMR guide. Where the rule holds and where it breaks

Nationally, the rule has room. The FY2027 2BR FMR median of 1,190 takes 17.5% of the US median household income, and even the ACS median of 1,490, which runs higher because half of all tenant leases sit above it, only reaches 21.9% (17,880 a year against 81,604). A median household that rents at the federal benchmark clears the 30% test with a wide margin.

The same test fails in the biggest metros, against their own incomes. New York's FMR median of 3,295 takes 39.6% of the metro's 99,852 median household income, and the income the rule demands, 131,800, sits 31,948 above what the median household earns. Miami needs 102,400 against an 80,625 median, a 21,775 gap. Los Angeles needs 118,400 against 96,405. San Francisco clears its own high income only because the income is high: 143,600 against 135,590 still busts the line by 8,010. Boston is the one metro in the table where the benchmark stays under 30%, at 28.7%.

Miami breaks the rule even without the FMR. Its 2024 ACS 2BR median gross rent of 2,149 takes 32.0% of the metro median income, so the rule fails against observed rents there, because incomes run near the national median while rents run coastal. New York runs the other direction: the ACS median of 1,887 takes only 22.7% of local income, because the median tenant signed a lease years ago at a lower rent, while the FMR prices what a recent mover faces today. Which rent you test decides which answer you get, and for the question how much rent can I afford, the recent-mover benchmark is the one a new lease will resemble.

What the ratios hide

Three limits, stated plainly. First, the income side counts all households, and renter households earn less than the all-household median, so the true rent-to-income ratio for a median renter runs higher than every figure on this page. Second, both rent benchmarks include a utilities allowance, so a contract rent that looks below the line can still push a household over once utilities land on top. Third, the clocks differ: FMRs are fiscal-year numbers effective October 1, and the ACS figures cover 2024, so rerun the arithmetic when either refreshes.

And the standing disclaimer: this page is arithmetic on published data. It is not financial advice, and it does not know your income, your utilities, or your lease.

Rent-to-income questions

How much rent can I afford on my income?
Multiply your gross monthly income by 0.30, or divide your annual income by 40. The 2024 US median household income was 81,604 (Census ACS one-year estimates), so the median household gets a monthly rent budget of 2,040 at the 30% line. Check the budget against real rents before trusting it: the FY2027 two-bedroom FMR median of 1,190 fits that budget, but the same benchmark in the New York metro runs 3,295, which needs 131,800 a year of income to stay at 30%.
What is a good rent-to-income ratio?
The standard cap is 0.30, meaning rent takes no more than 30% of gross income, monthly rent divided by monthly income. HUD counts households paying more than 30% of income for housing as cost-burdened, a threshold that traces to the 1969 Brooke Amendment, which capped public housing rents at 25% of income before Congress raised the figure to 30% in 1981. Tested against federal data, the national ratio sits at 17.5% (the FY2027 2BR FMR median of 1,190 against the 81,604 median household income), while the New York metro lands at 39.6% on the same test.
Does the 30% rule work in expensive cities?
Against the FY2027 2BR FMR median, the rule fails in four of five large coastal metros: New York 39.6%, Miami 38.1%, Los Angeles 36.8%, San Francisco 31.8% of each metro's own median household income. Boston is the only one under, at 28.7%. Miami breaks the rule even on observed rents: its 2024 ACS 2BR median gross rent of 2,149 takes 32.0% of the metro median income of 80,625. The ratios use all-household incomes, and renter households earn less, so real rent-to-income ratios run higher still.
