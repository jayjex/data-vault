Rural population share by country: the G20 from 1960 to 2025

World Bank WDI series SP.RUR.TOTL.ZS, retrieved 2026-09-10 (WDI update 2026-07-13), published 2026-09-10. Data source: World Bank Open Data, CC BY 4.0. Data Vault is not affiliated with the World Bank. This page documents the data; it is not demographic or policy advice.

What this page measures

Every number here is recomputed from the free CSV behind World Bank Rural Population Share, G20 Countries + EU27: 1,320 rows, one per entity-year, covering the 19 national G20 members plus the EU27 aggregate from 1960 through 2025. The column is rural_pct, the rural share of total population at the World Bank's own precision, nothing rounded or imputed. Headcounts are derived: rural_pct times the population column in the companion population pack, which covers the same entities and years.

Two conventions run through the page. The G20 seats the European Union alongside the 19 countries, and the EU27 aggregate is not a country, so club counts cover the 19 national members only. And each country's "rural" follows its own census definition, so the threshold is not harmonized; comparisons across countries are directionally solid, single-point differences are not.

India is the last majority-rural member

Eight of the 19 members were majority rural in 1960: China, India, Indonesia, Brazil, South Korea, Saudi Arabia, South Africa, and Turkiye. By the 2025 row, one remains. India's rural share stands at 64.31 percent, which works out to 941,424,277 people against its 1,463,865,525 total. That rural population alone is bigger than the entire United States by about 600 million people.

India's line moves slowly. It has shed 17.76 points since 1960 (82.07 to 64.31), second-smallest among the eight that started above half, behind only South Africa's 17.12, and it first slipped under 65 percent in 2023. At the recent pace of roughly a quarter point a year, the file ends before the rural majority does.

The others left majority-rural territory in a slow parade: Saudi Arabia in 1971, South Korea in 1977, Turkiye in 1989, then China and Indonesia in the same year, 2011. Indonesia is now the second-most rural member at 40.61 percent, about 116 million people, and South Africa follows at 36.18 percent.

The big drops since 1960

Urbanization in this set is a one-way street with one speed each. The steepest declines from 1960 to 2025, in percentage points of rural share:

Turkiye's fall is the deepest and also the fastest late: its steepest decade is 2005 to 2015, when the share went from 36.67 percent to 12.78 percent, 23.9 points in ten years. Saudi Arabia's came earliest, from 68.56 percent rural in 1960 to under half by 1971, driven by the oil-era move into cities. Indonesia's 85.39 percent start is the highest rural share anywhere in the file.

India anchors the slow end. Its 17.76-point drop since 1960 is the smallest among the eight that began majority rural, and the developed members barely move at all: the United Kingdom shed 4.8 points over the whole 65 years, Australia 6.1, Germany 6.4.

The two reversals

Not every line falls forever, and the file holds exactly two counterexamples. South Korea's rural share bottomed at 18.03 percent in 2010 and has crept up since, to 18.83 percent by 2025. The United States bottomed the same year at 19.30 percent and stood at 19.81 percent in 2025. They are the only two of the 19 whose 2025 value sits above their 2010 value; set the bar at 2000 and nobody clears it.

The rises are small, a fraction of a point a decade, and the World Bank's per-country rural definitions can shift with census practice, so both are best read as flat lines with a slight upward tilt rather than a counter-urbanization trend. For comparison, the peaks: 18 of the 20 entities recorded their highest rural share in 1960 itself, with China peaking in 1963 (83.16 percent) and the United Kingdom in 1971 (23.10 percent) as the only exceptions.

Read the series yourself

The full 1,320-row file downloads free on the rural share dataset page under CC BY 4.0, alongside a 20-row latest-year file (g20-rural-2025.csv) and a schema. Four columns: entity, iso3, year, rural_pct. Join it to the population pack on iso3 and year to reproduce every headcount above, and filter out EUU when you want national-only sums. If you republish derived numbers, the dataset license guide covers what CC BY 4.0 attribution asks of you.

The population side of the same file is its own story: the world population milestones guide reads the companion CSV for the India-passes-China crossover, the billion and 100-million crossings, and the six members already past their population peak.

Rural population share questions

Which G20 countries are still majority rural?
One: India. Its rural population share stands at 64.31 percent in the 2025 World Bank figures, about 941 million people out of 1,463,865,525. Every other member fell below 50 percent rural years ago; China and Indonesia both crossed the line in 2011, and Indonesia is now the second-most rural at 40.61 percent.
When did China become majority urban?
In 2011. China's rural share fell to 48.17 percent that year from 50.77 percent in 2010, and it has kept falling to 33.66 percent by 2025, about 473 million people still rural out of 1,406,585,000. Indonesia crossed the same line the same year, at 49.52 percent rural in 2011.
Which countries saw their rural share rise again?
Two, both off a 2010 bottom: South Korea's rural share bottomed at 18.03 percent in 2010 and has crept up to 18.83 percent by 2025, and the United States bottomed at 19.30 percent in 2010 and stood at 19.81 percent in 2025. They are the only members of the 19 whose 2025 share sits above their 2010 value; nobody is above their 2000 value.
