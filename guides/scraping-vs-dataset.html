Web scraping vs buying a dataset: the 5-factor decision

Published 2026-09-10. The three case studies below are datasets built and shipped on this site, with the numbers from those builds. This page is not legal advice.

Scrape or buy data: the question to ask first

Both routes end the same way: rows on your disk. The difference is who absorbs the work between the first run and the day the project ends. Scraping moves that work to you, one site redesign at a time. A ready dataset moves it to whoever cleaned and republished the file.

Before any of the five factors, answer one question: does a legal, downloadable source already exist? If yes, stop here, download it, and skip the scraper. The NFL case below shows why. If no, or if the source exists in a format that fights you, the five factors decide it.

The decision matrix: 5 factors

FactorScrape it yourselfReady dataset
Time to first rowHours: install, configure, test selectors per siteMinutes: download a file that already parses
MaintenanceEvery redesign breaks selectors; each fix is a small edit, repeated for the life of the projectNone. The file is fixed; a new release is a re-download
Legal / ToSDepends on the site and country. Terms of service may bar it, and courts in the US and EU treat the same scrape differentlyThe publisher's license states what you may do, in writing
FreshnessAs fresh as your last run; you control the cadenceAs fresh as the release date; you get what was published
VolumeOne site at a time, bounded by rate limits and page countsThousands of rows in one file, already deduped and typed

Two rows carry the most weight in practice. Maintenance is the one people underestimate: the first scraper is the cheap part. Legality is the one people overestimate for public data in the US and underestimate in the EU, where the same act gets weighed against privacy law. Airbnb data by country shows the effect on real coverage: scraping volunteers concentrate where the law is comfortable.

When scraping is the right call

Scrape when four things line up: no downloadable source exists, the pages are public, your freshness need matches a cadence you can run, and the scope is a handful of sites. A price you want to watch daily fits. A feed that loads on scroll fits. Both are what the scrape without coding templates cover, one config edit per site, with the robots.txt and rate-limit checks built in. Writing in Python instead? The same ten patterns map one to one in the python scraping templates guide.

When a ready dataset wins

A dataset wins when the job is recurring, the volume is large, or the legal question matters. Load the same source every week, join it, and chart it, and every manual step in your pipeline is a step you pay for again. This is the same trade as CSV vs API: a static file for reproducible loads, a query layer for ad hoc pulls.

The hidden case is data that needs cleaning no matter what. If the official source ships Excel workbooks with merge cells and text-stored numbers, someone has to do that conversion once. Doing it yourself is fine once. Paying for a file where it is already done is cheaper the second time you need it.

Three case studies from this site

Each dataset sold here started as a scrape-or-buy decision, and all three landed in a different place. The numbers come from the builds.

Case 1: HUD rent data, official but hostile

HUD publishes Fair Market Rents as free, public-domain Excel workbooks on huduser.gov. No scraping involved and none needed: the files download directly. The problem is the format. FY26_FMRs.xlsx holds 4,764 county rows across 14 columns, and the ZIP-level numbers live in a separate 4.2 MB workbook, fy2026_safmrs.xlsx. Real parsing problems from the conversion behind the HUD FMR data download guide: a header cell containing the literal string ZIP\nCode with a newline inside it, the county workbook refusing to open because of a malformed internal timestamp that took an unzip-edit-rezip to fix, and ZIP codes that lose their leading zeros the moment a spreadsheet tool reads them as numbers. The cleaned result, fmr-by-zip-2026.csv, is 51,895 rows with 10 typed columns, ZIPs preserved as text.

The lesson: an official free source removes the legality question and still leaves the format work. That work is a one-time cost, which is exactly what makes a cleaned HUD FMR CSV worth having instead of repeating the xlsx fight every fiscal year.

Case 2: Airbnb listings, legality decides it

Scraping Airbnb directly is off the table here. The company's terms of service bar scraping, and the law around it runs on different tracks: hiQ Labs v. LinkedIn (2022) left public-page scraping on solid ground in the US, while EU jurisdictions weigh the same act against GDPR. Instead, this site builds from Inside Airbnb snapshots, which volunteers collect and republish under CC BY 4.0. The 6-city pack is 90,169 listings across Austin, Nashville, Denver, New York City, Las Vegas, and San Diego, from snapshots taken between 2026-06-22 and 2026-08-10, normalized into one 12-column schema.

The lesson: where scraping is legally or contractually messy, the snapshot dataset is the legal route, and the price is freshness. A snapshot is one day's capture, and the methodology guide covers what that misses. For most analysis, a dated snapshot with a clean license beats a fresh scrape with a terms-of-service problem.

Case 3: NFL games, free legal source makes scraping pointless

nflverse publishes every NFL game since 1999 as free CSVs under CC BY 4.0, refreshed by automated GitHub Actions releases: 7,548 games in this site's pack, 46 columns per row, closing spreads and totals included. Scraping ESPN or a sports site would add a legal question, selector maintenance, and rate limits for data that is already free, licensed, and machine-readable. The nflverse comparison guide covers the one decision left: raw releases versus a preprocessed pack with normalized lines and derived tables, 861 team-season records and 27 season summaries.

The lesson: when a source publishes clean downloads under a permissive license, scraping is the wrong tool at any price. Check for the free source before writing the first selector.

Run the decision in order

  1. A legal, downloadable source exists? Download it. Done. (NFL)
  2. The source is legal but the format is hostile? Convert once, or take a cleaned file. (HUD)
  3. No source exists, and scraping the site is contractually or legally messy? Look for a republished dataset with a real license. (Airbnb)
  4. No source, scraping is fine, small scope, ongoing freshness need? Scrape. (the templates)
  5. Still torn? Count the maintenance: number of sites times redesigns per year, and weigh that against a one-line download.

FAQ: scraping or buying data

Is it cheaper to scrape data or buy a dataset?

Scraping costs time instead of money, and the time bill is recurring. The first run of a scraper is hours. The maintenance is forever, because every site redesign breaks selectors and each fix is a small edit that has to be repeated. A dataset is one download and zero selectors. For a one-off pull of a few hundred rows, scraping is usually the faster route. For anything you refresh weekly or load by the thousand-row file, the dataset wins on total time.

When should you scrape instead of using a dataset?

Four conditions, roughly all true at once: no downloadable source for the data exists, the pages are public, you need the freshness that your own runs give you, and the scope is a handful of sites you can check by hand. A price you want to watch daily or a small public table that no one republishes fit. A 50,000-row archive with a fixed release date does not, because the release already exists and scraping adds work without adding freshness.

Why not scrape Airbnb directly?

Airbnb's terms of service bar scraping, and the legal picture changes by jurisdiction: US courts have treated public-page scraping as mostly permissible since hiQ Labs v. LinkedIn in 2022, while EU jurisdictions weigh the same act against GDPR. Instead of scraping, this site builds from Inside Airbnb snapshots, which volunteers collect and republish under CC BY 4.0. The trade is freshness, the 6-city pack spans snapshots from 2026-06-22 to 2026-08-10, and the gain is a dataset with a clean license and no scraping liability.
