Section 8 voucher rent: how much the voucher pays, worked with FY2026 numbers

FY2026 edition, published 2026-09-10. Data source: HUD, public domain. Data Vault is not affiliated with HUD. This page documents the data and the arithmetic; it is not housing or legal advice, and your housing agency's calculation is the one that counts.

The voucher math in one look

Two numbers decide what a Housing Choice Voucher (Section 8) pays on any given lease. One is yours: the household's monthly adjusted income, of which you pay about 30% toward rent. The other belongs to your area: the payment standard, the figure your public housing agency (PHA) sets between 90% and 110% of HUD's fair market rent for the unit size. The voucher fills the space between those two numbers.

your share      = 30% of monthly adjusted income
voucher pays    = payment standard − your share
payment standard = between 90% and 110% of the FMR

That is why the same voucher program produces a different dollar amount for every household. The payment standard is fixed for your area and bedroom size; the income side moves with each family. Where that standard comes from, computed band-first from a real FY2026 metro, is in payment standard for Section 8; the concept-level comparison with the FMR itself is in HUD payment standard.

Worked example: Abilene, TX, two bedrooms

Start with a real row from the FY2026 FMR file. ZIP 76437 in Abilene, Texas (Abilene, TX MSA) carries these FY2026 figures, straight from the HUD FMR pack:

The two-bedroom FMR is 1,090 dollars, so the payment standard for that bedroom size must sit between 981 dollars (90%) and 1,199 dollars (110%). The Abilene agency's actual figure lives in its administrative plan, not in HUD's file. For the rest of this example, assume the agency set the payment standard at 1,090 dollars, level with the FMR, which is a legal choice inside the band.

Now add a household. The income figures below are examples, picked to show the mechanics; no real household is described. They also treat adjusted income as equal to gross income, which the real calculation does not: HUD deductions for dependents, childcare, and medical costs shrink the base before the 30% is taken.

Read the table left to right: the lower the income, the more the voucher covers, and the total stays pinned at the payment standard. At 3,633 dollars of monthly adjusted income, the 30% share alone reaches 1,090 dollars and the voucher contribution for this unit size drops to nothing. Federal rules also let an agency set a minimum tenant rent, capped at 50 dollars, so the lowest-income households in the table would pay the agency's minimum rather than the full 30% figure if that minimum were higher.

What happens when the unit rents for more than the standard

Say the two-bedroom unit you want asks 1,150 dollars a month and the agency approves it, even though the payment standard is 1,090 dollars. The voucher does not stretch. It still pays payment standard minus your share, and you pay the 60-dollar difference on top. At the 2,000-dollar example income, that means 600 dollars plus 60 dollars, or 660 dollars out of pocket. At lease-up, your total share including that difference cannot go past 40% of your monthly adjusted income, a federal guardrail; after you move in, a rent increase or income drop can push the effective share higher, and the annual recertification is where the agency recalculates.

Utilities complicate the split a little: the agency subtracts a utility allowance from the unit's rent-plus-utilities cost before comparing it to the payment standard. In practice the voucher can cover part of the utility bill when the allowance is large. The agency's schedule lists the allowance per bedroom size.

Where each number comes from

The FMR side is public and national: HUD publishes it each fiscal year for every metro, county, and designated ZIP from American Community Survey data, and FMR by ZIP code shows how to look up any single ZIP in the FY2026 file. The payment standard side is local: each of the roughly 3,000 agencies picks a point in the 90%-110% band, per bedroom size, and publishes it in its schedule (how that band works). Before any of this math applies, the household has to clear the program's income eligibility test, which runs on a different HUD dataset altogether; HUD income limits covers how the two scales differ.

Section 8 rent questions

How much does Section 8 pay toward rent?
The voucher pays the gap between your share and the area's payment standard. Your share is generally 30% of monthly adjusted income, and the payment standard is the figure your public housing agency sets between 90% and 110% of HUD's fair market rent. Example with FY2026 numbers: in Abilene, TX, ZIP 76437, the two-bedroom FMR is 1,090 dollars. Assume the agency set the payment standard at that figure and a household's monthly adjusted income is 2,000 dollars. The household pays 600 dollars (30%) and the voucher covers 490 dollars. Every household in the same unit size gets a different voucher number, because the income side changes while the payment standard stays fixed.
How is my Section 8 rent share calculated?
Your agency starts from your income after HUD-allowed deductions (childcare, medical costs above a threshold, and dependents all reduce the figure), then takes roughly 30% of the adjusted monthly amount as your share of rent and utilities. Federal rules let an agency set a minimum tenant rent, capped at 50 dollars. If the unit's rent and utility allowance together exceed the payment standard, you pay the difference on top of your 30% share. The agency calculates the exact number at your annual recertification, so your portion moves when your income moves, not when the market does.
Does a Section 8 voucher cover the full rent?
A voucher never covers more than the payment standard minus your 30% share, and it never follows the asking price upward. If a two-bedroom unit rents for 1,150 dollars in an area where the payment standard is 1,090 dollars, the voucher still pays the same amount and you pay the extra 60 dollars, as long as the agency approves the unit. At lease-up, your total share including that difference cannot exceed 40% of your adjusted monthly income. In the Abilene example with 2,000 dollars monthly adjusted income, the voucher covers 490 dollars of the 1,150-dollar unit and the household pays 660 dollars.
